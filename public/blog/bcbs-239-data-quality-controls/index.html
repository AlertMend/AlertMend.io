What does BCBS 239 require for data quality?

BCBS 239 is the Basel Committee standard for how banks aggregate and report risk data. It was published in January 2013 as Principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting, aimed first at global systemically important banks with a compliance date of January 2016. Many supervisors now expect domestic systemically important banks to follow it too.

The standard groups 14 principles into four areas. For a data team, the daily work sits in the risk data aggregation principles, 3 to 6, plus report accuracy in principle 7. Those are the ones that translate into things you can actually measure on a table.

  • Governance and infrastructure, principles 1 and 2: board oversight and data architecture.
  • Risk data aggregation, principles 3 to 6: accuracy and integrity, completeness, timeliness, adaptability.
  • Risk reporting, principles 7 to 11: report accuracy, comprehensiveness, clarity, frequency, distribution.
  • Supervisory review, principles 12 to 14: how supervisors review, remediate and cooperate.
The requirement is proof, not paper

Most banks already have a written data quality policy mapped to BCBS 239. The hard part is proving every day that the policy is enforced, and being able to show the evidence on demand. At the 2020 progress review the Basel Committee reported that full compliance across G-SIBs had still not been met, years after the deadline.

Which BCBS 239 principles become data quality checks?

Four aggregation and reporting principles map cleanly onto concrete check types. The mapping is the centerpiece of a traceable control framework: an auditor can read the principle, then the check, then the last result, in one line.

The check catalog behind this is not exotic. In practice: A late nightly load, traced to the clause it broke

At 06:12 the nightly ODI load stops on an Oracle error, ORA-01400, and leaves BANKING.CUSTOMER_ACCOUNTS with duplicate account_id rows.

The uniqueness check on account_id fails, reads 98.7% against an expected 100%, and caps the dataset quality score so a healthy average cannot hide it.

The Teams alert names the job nightly_load, lists the three Power BI reports that read the table, and shows the policy line the check enforces: BCBS 239, Principle 3, accuracy and integrity.

When a check carries its clause reference, a single failure is already audit evidence: the auditor sees the principle, the control, the cause and the affected reports without a spreadsheet.

Why do data quality policies stay on paper?

The policy is rarely the problem. The gap opens between the policy and the running controls, and it opens in predictable ways.

  • Someone has to turn every clause into a rule by hand, so most policies end up only partly monitored.
  • Rules drift from the policy over time, and no one can say which clause a given rule still enforces.
  • When an auditor asks how a principle is enforced, the answer is days of spreadsheet archaeology.
  • Failures are often found by business users who spot a wrong number in a report before the data team does.
The quarterly evidence trap

Gathering clause-to-control evidence once a quarter tells you the state of the world on the day you gathered it, not today. Between reviews the link between policy and control is unverified, which is exactly the window an auditor will ask about.

The fix is to close the paper-to-control gap once, with a repeatable method rather than a one-off project. Making each check traceable to its clause for audit

Traceability is a property you design in, not a report you generate later. Six habits turn monitoring into audit evidence.

  1. Map clauses to checks
    For each policy clause, list the tables and columns it covers and the check types that enforce it: completeness, uniqueness, validity, freshness, volume, referential integrity.
  2. Keep the clause reference on the check
    Every check records which clause it enforces. This single field is what turns a passing check into audit evidence and a failing check into a citation.
  3. Approve before go-live
    A data owner reviews and approves each check, and the approval is recorded with a name and a timestamp. Nothing goes live unapproved.
  4. Alert with cause and impact
    When a check fails, the alert names the pipeline job that caused it and the reports that read the table, so the failure is actionable and traceable at once.
  5. Version every change
    Checks change as the business changes. Keep a history of who changed what and why, and keep the ability to roll a check back to a prior version.
  6. Score so failures cannot hide
    An average quality score can look healthy while a critical check fails. Cap the score whenever any critical check is failing.
What good looks like

An auditor asks how Principle 3 is enforced on BANKING.CUSTOMER_ACCOUNTS. You show the uniqueness check on account_id, the clause it cites, the data owner who approved it, its version history, and the last hundred runs with results. No spreadsheet, no scramble.
