What GPU monitoring must catch

A GPU is the most expensive thing in your cluster, and it bills by the hour whether it is training a model or sitting at zero percent utilization. That single fact is why GPU monitoring is different from ordinary monitoring: the goal is not only to know a GPU is upbut to know it is earning its costand to do something the moment it is not.

Three failures waste GPU money and reliability, and a good setup catches all three. An idle GPU is allocated but doing no work. A stalled GPU is worse: the pod looks Runningbut the training step hung or the inference queue stopped, so it burns money while producing nothing. And a crashed GPU, a CUDA out-of-memory, a thermal throttle, a driver fault at 3am, takes the workload down until someone notices. Monitoring that only charts utilization tells you these happened; monitoring that acts stops them.

The signals that matter

GPU health is more than one number. These are the signals that separate a busy GPU from an expensive idle one, and a healthy one from a failing one.

How to diagnose it

1

Read real utilization

Run nvidia-smi --query-gpu=utilization.gpu,memory.used,temperature.gpu,power.draw --format=csv or dcgmi dmon. Sustained low utilization on an allocated GPU is the signal, whatever the pod says.

2

Tie the GPU to its workload

Find which pod holds the GPU (kubectl describe node shows nvidia.com/gpu allocation) and confirm that workload is actually doing GPU work, not just running.

3

Check for stalls mid-run

For training, watch step time and tokens/s; a flatline while the process lives is a stall (dataloader, NCCL, or checkpoint), not a healthy idle.

4

Rule out hardware

Check temperature, SM clock, ECC, and Xid errors in dmesg or DCGM. Thermal throttle and hardware faults masquerade as "slow" rather than "down".

The three problems, and what to do

Idle GPU, still billing

The GPU is allocated but utilization sits near zero, a finished job that never released, an oversized reservation, or a forgotten notebook, while it costs full price per hour.

Action: Alert on utilization below a floor for a window, then notify the owner and reclaim: scale the workload down, preempt it, or free the reservation.

Stalled workload (pod up, GPU idle)

The pod is Running but the GPU stopped working: a hung dataloader, an NCCL timeout, or a stuck inference queue. A node-up check stays green while the run produces nothing.

Action: Detect utilization dropping to zero mid-run and restart the worker, or resume training from the last checkpoint.

Crash or OOM at 3am

A CUDA out-of-memory, a thermal throttle, or a driver fault kills the worker while the API in front still looks fine, and nobody is awake to restart it.

Action: Auto-recover: restart the worker, cordon a throttling or faulting node, and evict a bad rank, then confirm it is serving again.

Underutilized inference

A model server holds a whole GPU but runs at low batch, or KV-cache pressure throttles throughput while utilization looks partial.

Action: Right-size with MIG or time-slicing to pack more work per GPU, and alert on throughput drift, not just up/down.

Thermal throttling

The GPU is busy but temperature is high and clocks are dropping, so it is slow rather than down, and jobs quietly take longer and cost more.

Action: Alert on temperature and SM-clock drop, and cordon the node so new jobs avoid it until cooling is fixed.

Hardware fault (GPU falls off)

ECC or Xid errors rise, or the GPU disappears from the node, taking any job scheduled there down with it.

Action: Alert on ECC/Xid, cordon the node automatically, and reschedule the workload onto healthy GPUs.

Set up GPU monitoring

The standard stack is NVIDIA DCGM exported to Prometheus, plus the GPU Operator to manage drivers and the device plugin. Then alert on utilization, memory, and thermal, not just node health.

# 1. NVIDIA GPU Operator installs drivers, device plugin, and DCGM exporter
helm repo add nvidia https://helm.ngc.nvidia.com/nvidia
helm install gpu-operator nvidia/gpu-operator -n gpu-operator --create-namespace

# 2. Confirm dcgm-exporter is exposing GPU metrics to Prometheus
kubectl get pods -n gpu-operator -l app=nvidia-dcgm-exporter

# 3. Alert on an idle GPU (utilization under 15% for 30m), PromQL
#    DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL is the per-GPU utilization gauge
avg_over_time(DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL[30m]) < 15

# 4. Alert on a stall: a pod holds the GPU (dcgm-exporter --kubernetes adds pod/namespace
#    labels) but utilization has been near zero for 10m, "Running" is not "working"
avg_over_time(DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL{pod!=""}[10m]) < 5

The rule that catches idle and stalled GPUs is the same: alert when utilization stays below a floor for a window, and separately when it drops mid-run. A pod that is Running is not evidence the GPU is working.

Prevent idle and silent stalls

The durable fixes are packing and timeouts. Use MIG or time-slicing so a single job cannot hoard a whole GPU, set idle timeouts on notebooks and dev jobs so forgotten sessions release the hardware, and make your health checks verify work (tokens/s, step progress), not just that the process is alive.

# Idle-timeout a Jupyter/dev workload with a liveness probe on real GPU work
# (pseudocode: fail the probe if utilization has been ~0 for N minutes)
livenessProbe:
  exec:
    command: ["/bin/sh","-c","test $(gpu_util_last_10m) -gt 3"]
  periodSeconds: 60
  failureThreshold: 10

Then alert on the two conditions that catch the expensive cases early: utilization below a floor for a sustained window (idle), and utilization dropping sharply mid-run while the workload still lives (stall).
