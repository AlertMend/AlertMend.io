How do you turn a data quality policy into checks?

Almost every regulated company has a data quality policy. Far fewer can show which parts of it are monitored today. The gap is not intent. It is the manual work of translating each clause into a rule, keeping the rules in step with the policy, and proving it to an auditor.

The method below is domain neutral. It works whether the policy governs banking customer records, healthcare claims or supplier master data, and whether you do it by hand or with tooling. For a fully worked regulated example, see the BCBS 239 walkthrough at /blog/bcbs-239-data-quality-controls.

  • Break the policy into testable clauses.
  • Map each clause to a check type from a known catalog.
  • Keep the clause reference on every check.
  • Approve, version and review each check before and after it goes live.
A rewrite is the first real step

Customer records must be complete is not testable. Every active customer has a non-empty address, date of birth and tax ID is. Rewrite each clause until a query could answer it with a yes or a no.

Step by step: from policy clause to live check

  1. Break the policy into testable clauses
    Read the policy clause by clause and rewrite each one as something a query can answer. For each clause note the dataset and columns it covers, the dimension it enforces, the threshold, and the owner who can approve it.
  2. Choose a check type per clause
    Map the wording to a dimension: completeness, uniqueness, validity, format, referential integrity, numeric range, volume, freshness, anomaly or trend. A single clause often maps to more than one check.
  3. Set the threshold from the policy, not a guess
    Start with the policy hard rules, for example zero duplicate account ids, or an allowed rate such as under 0.5 percent missing. Add history-based anomaly and trend checks only once a dataset has enough history.
  4. Keep the clause reference on the check
    Store the clause id on every check, for example Policy 4.2 or BCBS 239 Principle 3. This one field is what lets you answer show me how this rule is enforced in seconds instead of days.
  5. Approve before anything goes live
    Route each new or changed check to the data owner, record the approval, and only then activate it. Checks written by one person and never reviewed become noise.
  6. Alert with cause and impact
    A failed check should name what failed and the observed value, the pipeline run that loaded the data with its error, and the reports and teams that read the table.
In practice: A KYC completeness clause becomes a live check

Clause 4.2 of the internal policy reads: every active customer record must have a non-empty address, date of birth and tax ID.

That single clause maps to three completeness checks on BANKING.CUSTOMER_ACCOUNTS, each tagged Policy 4.2.

The data owner approves all three, they go live, and a later failure alerts Teams with the clause the check enforces.

One vague sentence often becomes several precise checks. Keeping the clause reference on each one is what turns monitoring into audit evidence.

How do you keep each check traceable to its clause?

Traceability is a single design decision made early: every check carries the clause it enforces as a first-class field, not a note buried in a script comment. Once that link exists, coverage becomes a report you can run. For any clause you can list the checks enforcing it, their history and their current state. For any check you can name the clause that justifies it.

The link also runs the other way, which is where gaps hide. Two lists matter at every review: clauses with no check yet, and checks with no clause. The first is unmonitored policy. The second is monitoring nobody asked for, which is usually where alert noise starts.

Averages hide failing checks

A 97 percent average quality score can hide one critical failing check. Let any failing critical check cap the score for that dataset, so the number never smooths over a real problem.

Approving, versioning and reviewing checks

Policies change, and so must checks. A threshold gets tightened, a clause is retired, a new dataset comes into scope. Without a version history you lose the one thing an auditor asks for: what was this check on the day the failure happened, and who changed it.

Treat every check like code. Each new or changed check is a proposal with an owner and a reason. Approvals are recorded. Previous versions are kept, and a bad edit to a check can be rolled back to the last approved version. Rolling back a check is not the same as changing your data, which the checks never touch.

  • Rules without owners: every check needs someone who can approve it and act when it fails.
  • Silent edits: record who changed a check, when and why, and keep the prior version.
  • Stale coverage: review clauses with no check and checks with no clause on a regular cadence.
