Before comparing products, it helps to be clear on what you are collecting and why, because the two halves of the job, logs and metrics, are not interchangeable. Getting the split right is what makes an incident fast to resolve instead of a guessing game.

Logs vs metrics: what each one is for

They answer different questions. A strong setup collects both and lets you jump between them.

Metrics

Numeric time series sampled over time: CPU, memory, request rate, error rate, latency. They are cheap to store, easy to chart, and ideal for trends and alert thresholds. Metrics are how you notice that something is wrong.

Logs

Timestamped, detailed records of individual events: a stack trace, a failed request, an out-of-memory kill. They are richer and heavier than metrics, and they are how you find out exactly why something went wrong.

The magic is in the correlation. A metric shows a latency spike at 14:32; the logs from 14:32 show the exact error behind it. Tools that keep logs and metrics together, and let you pivot from one to the other, turn a thirty-minute investigation into a two-minute one. Tools that split them across products make every incident slower.

How we evaluated the tools

A transparent rubric makes this comparison repeatable and easier to challenge.

1. First useful signal

We count the required onboarding decisions: backend deployment, collector or agent, credentials, configuration, storage, and dashboard work. We do not pretend every environment takes the same number of minutes.

2. Signal coverage

Native support for logs and metrics, plus whether traces, events, and APM are part of the same investigation workflow.

3. Operating burden

Who owns scaling, upgrades, retention, high availability, and collector health: your team, the vendor, or a mixture of both.

4. Investigation workflow

How easily a responder can move from a symptom to relevant logs, traces, topology, or a probable root cause.

5. Portability

Support for OpenTelemetry and open query or storage conventions, balanced against proprietary agents, schemas, and workflows.

6. Actionability

Whether the product stops at visualization and notification or can safely trigger a controlled remediation workflow.

7. Enterprise deployment

Managed and self-hosted options, data-boundary control, access governance, and an operating model that can fit production security requirements.

What to look for in a logging and metrics tool

Rate every option against these. The more it covers, the less monitoring work lands on your team.

Setup effort

How long from signing up to a useful signal. Config files, agents, and storage to operate all add friction; connecting a cluster with none of that is the fast path.

Both logs and metrics

One tool that covers both, so you are not stitching two products together during an incident.

Correlation

The ability to jump from a metric spike straight to the logs from that moment, which is where diagnosis time is won or lost.

Alerting with context

Alerts that carry root cause, not a wall of raw notifications you still have to interpret.

Retention and cost

Logs get expensive fast. Predictable retention and pricing matter as much as features once you are at scale.

Does it act, or just report?

Most tools stop at a dashboard and an alert. The strongest also act on the data, for example by auto-recovering a failed service.

Is it developer friendly?

Setup speed gets you started. The bigger question is ongoing: when a service breaks, does a normal developer get the answer they need, or a pile of raw data and a query language to learn first?

There are two philosophies here. Query-first tools hand you powerful raw data and expect you to write queries and build dashboards to get value from it. Answer-first tools hand you the conclusion. Query-first is unbeatable for open-ended exploration if you have the time or a platform team to lean on; answer-first wins when a developer just needs to know what broke and how to fix it, fast. Neither is wrong, and the right one depends on who is on call at 2am.

Onboarding a new service

Query-first: add exporters or instrumentation, then build a dashboard before you see anything. Answer-first: connect it and the signals show up.

A 2am incident

Query-first: open dashboards, write a query, correlate logs and metrics by hand. Answer-first: read the root cause in the alert and move.

Do I need a platform team?

Query-first stacks usually need someone to own, tune, and operate them. Answer-first tools stay self-serve for a small team.

Best practices for logging and metrics

A few habits keep an observability setup useful instead of noisy and expensive.

Collect both, correlate always

Metrics to notice, logs to explain. Keep them in one place so you can pivot from a spike to the logs behind it without switching tools.

Alert on symptoms, not everything

Alert on what users feel, like errors and latency, and suppress the rest. Noisy alerting is where on-call trust goes to die.

Control log volume and retention

Logs are the expensive half. Set sensible retention, sample high-volume debug logs, and keep the signal that actually helps an incident.

Make the data act

A dashboard nobody watches at 2am helps no one. Wire alerts to root cause and, where safe, to automatic recovery so signals lead to fixes.
