What are the main types of data quality checks?

Data quality checks look endless when you add them one incident at a time. They are not. Almost every check you will ever write falls into one of 10 categories, and knowing the categories lets you cover a table on purpose instead of by accident.

Think of the categories in two groups. Structural checks ask whether a row is well formed on its own: is a value present, unique, in the allowed set, in the right format, in a sane range, and does it point at a record that exists. Behavioral checks ask whether the dataset as a whole is behaving: did it arrive on time, did the row count look normal, do related totals reconcile, and is anything drifting away from its own history.

  • Completeness, uniqueness, validity, format, referential integrity, and numeric range are the per-row structural checks.
  • Volume, freshness, consistency, and anomaly or trend are the dataset-level behavioral checks.

Anomaly checks need history

A trend or anomaly check learns from each dataset's own past, so it needs enough history before it can judge. A good implementation waits rather than guessing on a table it has only seen for two days. Lead with structural checks, then let anomaly checks warm up.

One category is deliberately absent from this list: watching for columns being dropped, renamed, or retyped. That is a structural signal about the table definition rather than the rows, and it is a separate discipline. AlertMend does not treat it as a data quality check today, so this taxonomy stays on the 10 row-and-dataset categories above.

In practice: One duplicate key, three reports, before 9am

The nightly_load job retries after a timeout and inserts a batch twice into BANKING.CUSTOMER_ACCOUNTS.

A uniqueness check on account_id fails at 06:12: uniqueness drops to 98.7 percent against an expected 100 percent.

The failing check names the load job that caused it and lists the three Power BI reports that read the table, so the steward fixes it before the 9am meeting.

A single uniqueness check on a key catches a class of retry and fan-out bugs that validity and range checks never see. Pick the check type to match the failure mode, not the column.

How do you prioritise which checks to run first?

You cannot put all 10 checks on every table on day one, and you should not try. Prioritise by blast radius: the tables that feed a decision or a regulated report earn the most checks, and everything else can wait.

  1. Start with freshness and volume
    Put these two on every table that feeds a critical report. They are cheap, they need no column knowledge, and they catch the loudest failures: a load that did not arrive or arrived half-empty.
  2. Add completeness and uniqueness on keys
    Guard the primary key and the mandatory fields next. Uniqueness on a key catches retry and fan-out duplicates that nothing else will see.
  3. Add validity, format, range, and referential integrity where the policy asks
    These per-row checks are where a written data quality policy usually has the most to say. Add them on the columns the policy names, not everywhere.
  4. Turn on anomaly and trend once tables have history
    Let the behavioral checks warm up on tables that already have a month or more of loads, so they judge against a real baseline instead of guessing.
Freshness deserves its own playbook

Late data is the failure business users notice first. For SLA windows, expected arrival times, and how to alert without paging on a slow-but-fine load, see /blog/data-freshness-monitoring.

How do you go from ad-hoc SQL to continuous checks?

The queries above are the right way to explore a table and prove a rule. They are the wrong way to run a data quality program, because a query in someone's history does not run tonight, does not know which report it protects, and does not tell anyone when it fails.

Turning ad-hoc SQL into a program means three things: the check runs on a schedule against live data, a failure names both the job that caused it and the report it affects, and the right people hear about it without being buried in noise.

  • Schedule: each check runs on its own cadence, not when someone remembers to paste SQL.
  • Cause and impact: a failed check points at the load job that broke it and the downstream reports that read the table.
  • Routing: alerts reach Slack or Teams with cooldowns and maintenance windows so a known outage does not page four times.
